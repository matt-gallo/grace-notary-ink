Do You Need Witnesses to Notarize a Document?

By Brenda S. Hill · September 7, 2026 · Mobile notary serving Boise & the Treasure Valley

Home › Blog › Do You Need Witnesses to Notarize a Document?

I have stood in more than one Boise living room at nine at night while a client knocked on a neighbor's door in their slippers. Not because anything went wrong — because we got to the signature line and found two small words nobody had read: witnesses required.

So here's the short answer, before you're in that spot. Most notarizations do not need witnesses. A handful do. And the ones that do will say so right on the page — usually in print small enough that people miss it until I'm already sitting at the table.

A notary and a witness are two different jobs

This trips up almost everybody, and it's a fair mistake to make, because both jobs involve watching you sign.

As your notary, I verify your identity, confirm you're signing willingly and know what you're signing, then record it with my seal, my signature, and an entry in my journal. That's an official act.

A witness is doing something simpler. A witness is just a person who was in the room, saw you sign, and puts their own name on the document to say so. They don't check ID. They don't have a commission. They're a set of eyes with a signature.

Because those are separate roles, one person usually cannot fill both on the same document. If your form calls for two witnesses, that means two people in addition to me.

How many witnesses does a document need?

Whatever the document says — and only the document can tell you. Most forms that require witnesses ask for two. Some ask for one. Many ask for none at all.

The documents where witness lines turn up most often in my work are estate and end-of-life paperwork, certain powers of attorney, advance directives and living wills, and occasional real estate or trust documents. Some of these vary by which state the document will be used in, which is exactly why I never guess on your behalf.

The reliable move: before your appointment, look at the signature page. If you see blank lines labeled Witness, you need witnesses. If you don't, you almost certainly don't.

An important limit: I can notarize your signature and make sure the signing is done correctly, but I can't tell you whether your document requires witnesses, how many it needs, or what any of its terms mean. Those are legal questions and they belong to an attorney or to whoever prepared the form. Nothing here is legal advice.

Who can — and can't — be your witness

Generally a witness needs to be an adult, mentally competent, present in the room for the actual signing, and holding valid photo ID if the document or I need to confirm who they are.

The bigger issue is who should not witness. As a rule, avoid anyone with a stake in the document — someone named in it, someone who inherits under it, someone being granted authority by it, and often the spouse of any of those people. A witness whose interest can be questioned later is the kind of detail that gets a document challenged years down the road, long after everyone has forgotten the evening it was signed.

And no, I can't be your witness. I'm already the notary.

Your witness checklist

Run through this the day before, not the hour of:

  • Check the signature page for lines marked Witness, and count how many.
  • Ask the document's source — the attorney, title company, or agency that gave it to you — if you're unsure who qualifies.
  • Line up neutral people. A neighbor, a coworker, a friend who isn't named anywhere in the paperwork.
  • Have them bring photo ID, unexpired and government-issued.
  • Confirm the time with them, not just with me. Witnesses have to be physically present for the signing, start to finish.
  • Leave every signature line blank — witnesses included. Nobody signs before I arrive.
