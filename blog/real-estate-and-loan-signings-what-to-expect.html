Real Estate & Loan Signings: What to Expect

By Brenda S. Hill · September 14, 2026 · Mobile notary serving Boise & the Treasure Valley

Home › Blog › Real Estate & Loan Signings: What to Expect

The first time I set a loan package on someone's kitchen table, they looked at the stack and asked if I'd brought the wrong file. A hundred and forty pages, for one house. Nobody warns you about the size of it.

So let me warn you now, and then take the fear out of it. A real estate or refinance signing is the most paper you will ever sign in one sitting, and it is also one of the most predictable appointments I do. The package looks the same from one closing to the next. Once you know the shape of it, the stack stops being scary.

What a loan signing agent actually does

When your loan is ready to close, the title or escrow company sends the documents to a signing agent instead of asking you to drive across town during business hours. That's me. I bring the package to your home, your office, or wherever you are in the Treasure Valley, and we go through it together.

My job has three parts: make sure every signature, initial, and date lands where the lender needs it, notarize the pages that require it, and get the completed package back to title on time so your loan funds. I don't work for the lender or the buyer. I'm the neutral person who makes sure the paperwork is done right.

What arrives in the package

Every loan is a little different, but you'll almost always see these:

  • The Closing Disclosure. The five-page summary of your loan terms and costs. You should have received it from your lender at least three business days before the signing, so nothing on it should surprise you.
  • The Note. Your promise to repay. It states the amount, the rate, and the payment. This one gets signed, not notarized.
  • The Deed of Trust. The document that ties the loan to the property. It's notarized, and it's usually the longest thing in the stack.
  • Affidavits and disclosures. Occupancy, identity, name variations, and a handful of statements from the lender and title company. Several are notarized.
  • Title company paperwork. Settlement statements, wire instructions, and the escrow instructions that tell everyone what happens after you sign.

If you're purchasing, there's a deed for the seller to sign as well. Sellers often have their own separate, shorter appointment.

A limit worth knowing before I arrive: I can tell you what a document is called and where to sign it. I can't explain what the terms mean, whether the numbers are right, or whether you should sign. Those questions belong to your loan officer, your title or escrow officer, or an attorney. If something on the page doesn't match what you were told, we pause and you call them from the table. That happens more than you'd think, and it's the right move every time.

How long does a loan signing take?

Plan on about an hour. A refinance with one borrower can be done in forty-five minutes. A purchase with two borrowers and a few extra lender forms runs closer to ninety. What stretches an appointment isn't the page count. It's a missing ID, a name that doesn't match the documents, or a question that has to wait for a callback from the lender.

One timing note for refinances on your primary home: federal rules generally give you three business days after signing to change your mind before the loan funds. Your lender can explain how that applies to you.

Your signing-day checklist

  • Unexpired, government-issued photo ID for every borrower. The name on it needs to match the name on the loan.
  • A second form of ID if your title company asked for one. Many do.
  • Your Closing Disclosure, so you can compare the numbers as we go.
  • Every signer present, including a non-borrowing spouse if title told you they need to sign.
  • A clear table and about an hour, with the TV off and the dog somewhere happy.
  • Your loan officer's phone number within reach.
  • Nothing signed ahead of time. Not one page.

If your document has witness lines, my post on when a notarization needs witnesses covers who qualifies. And the general rules in preparing documents before a signing apply here too.
